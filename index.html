Research note

Shuffling the frames

A simple test of which microbubble tracks in a transcranial ultrasound recording are real. It also showed why the tracker misses most of the brain below 3 cm, and how to fix that.

Amulya Harish · October 2026 · 8 min read

Following bubbles through the skull

Ultrasound can map blood vessels through the skull if it has something bright to follow. Microbubbles, a few microns across and injected into the blood, echo far more strongly than tissue. Find each bubble in every frame, join its positions from frame to frame, and the joined tracks trace the vessels. The method is called ultrasound localization microscopy, or ULM.

At the Axo Neurotech hackathon in August, Aleph Neuro shared two ULM recordings of a human head, made with a handheld probe of 68 × 8 elements at 2.4 MHz. One looks in through the temple for about a minute at 110 frames per second. The other looks in through the back of the head for 28 seconds at 177 frames per second. Aleph also published the software that turns these recordings into tracks.1

I set out to improve the images. I ended up testing the tracks instead.

A gain that wasn’t real

My first idea was to weight every point of the image by how well the probe’s 544 elements agree about it. Bubbles make the elements agree, and noise does not. With the weighting, Aleph’s tracker reported 30 percent more tracks, and the new tracks lay along the same vessels.

Then I ran a control that should have failed: the same weighting, deliberately misplaced. A mirror image of the weight map gave 2,846 tracks against 1,889 for the correct one. A randomly scrambled map gave 5,089. The track count rewarded any weighting, and a wrong one most of all, so I withdrew the result. Before improving anything, I needed a way to tell real tracks from chance.

The shuffle test

A real track is one bubble moving a short distance from each frame to the next. Put the frames in a random order and that becomes impossible, because a bubble cannot jump between moments seconds apart. Yet every frame is still the same picture, so the software finds exactly the same bright dots. Any track it still reports after shuffling joins dots that have nothing to do with each other.

The test needs no answer key and costs one extra run of the tracker. I ran it over both full recordings, 512 frames at a time, with the settings the software’s README recommends.2

For the first half of the temple recording the two counts match, block after block, so every track there is chance. Most of them sit on one bright, flickering spot just under the skull. About 30 seconds in, many more bubbles arrive and the tracks turn clean. The acquisition settings stay the same throughout, so the change comes from the signal itself. The back-of-head recording stays mostly chance from start to finish.

The practice files handed out at the hackathon were the first 1,024 frames of the temple recording, which sit entirely inside the chance-only stretch. With the simpler fixed settings I had been using, 48 to 89 percent of all tracks of 5 or more points were chance, depending on the recording and the stretch of it.

One threshold for every depth

The cause sits in how the software decides what counts as a bubble. It sets one brightness threshold for each slice of the volume, shared by every depth. In these recordings the background noise is about ten times stronger just under the skull than 5 cm down. The software has an optional step that evens out brightness with depth, and it was not used when these files were made.

Near the skull the threshold sits at or below the noise, so noise passes as bubbles and chance links it into tracks. Below 3 cm on the temple recording it sits 4 to 6 times above the noise, so real bubbles are missed.

To check, I planted synthetic bubbles with known paths and brightness into the real recordings.3 A planted bubble six times brighter than the noise around it was found in 87 percent of frames 1 to 2 cm deep, and never 4 to 6 cm deep.

Evening out the depth, after the clean-up

The obvious fix is the software’s own depth-levelling step. Used where the software applies it, while the image is formed and before the clutter filter removes tissue echo, it opens up the deep half but loses about half of the shallow tracks.

So I moved the same levelling to after the clutter filter, where it changes only the threshold. I ran Aleph’s clutter filter myself, levelled the cleaned image, and passed it to the unmodified tracker with its own filter switched off. Without levelling, this route reproduces the normal results exactly.

Tracks that travel, over both full recordings: at least 10 points, at least 2 mm from start to end, and mostly straight. Each cell gives the count above 3 cm / below 3 cm. In shuffled order the counts below 3 cm stay at 8 or fewer.

Levelling after the clutter filter keeps about three quarters of the shallow tracks, and it finds 3 times as many deep ones through the temple and 19 times as many through the back of the head. With the recommended settings it beats the shipped files at both depths on both recordings. With the fixed settings the shipped temple files still give a few more travelling tracks overall, 844 against 775, so the trade-off shrinks without disappearing.

What the tracker does well

The planted bubbles also measure what happens once a bubble is found. The software places it to within about 0.1 mm, a seventh of the sound’s wavelength, at every depth. It also gets speed right: bubbles planted at 2, 20 and 80 mm per second came back at 2.3, 20.0 and 80.0. The weak steps are finding the bubbles and linking them up.

Along the way

Two smaller results came out of the earlier work. I rebuilt the software’s image-forming step so it runs on a laptop, and it reproduces the shipped images at a correlation of 0.994. And the skull turned out not to be the main limit on image quality. The assumed speed of sound is right to within 25 m/s, and a correction pooled over 30,000 bubble positions finds nothing useful near the skull and about 1 dB to gain below 2.6 cm.4

Limits

  • Two recordings of one person, made on one day.
  • The shuffle test catches chance links. It does not catch something that flickers in place for a second, such as leftover tissue echo, which is why the depth comparison counts only tracks that travel.
  • Passing these tests does not prove that a track is a bubble.
  • The levelling was tested with one formula, and the planted bubbles are idealised blobs rather than simulated echoes.

What I would suggest

  1. Report the shuffled count next to every track count. It costs one extra run.
  2. Even out the image by depth after the clutter filter, or set the threshold locally in depth.
  3. Treat the first 28 seconds of the temple recording, practice files included, as nearly empty under the recommended settings.

Notes

  1. Data: the Hackathon Ultrasound Dataset shared by Aleph Neuro, recordings temporal-20260812-045532 and occipital-20260812-053159, beamformed on a one-wavelength grid. Software: alephneuro/microbubbles at commit ae169ea, used unmodified. For background on the method, see Aleph’s post Ultrasound imaging of the brain.
  2. Recommended settings: the adaptive SVD clutter filter with the knee filter, threshold z > 2, Kalman linking. On these recordings the adaptive filter always falls back to removing 10 percent of components, because its 100 Hz tissue cutoff is above the frame rate’s Nyquist limit. Fixed settings: 8 SVD components removed, z > 2, the default linking distance of 1.34 mm per frame.
  3. 600 synthetic bubbles per recording, each a Gaussian blob with widths measured from real isolated bubbles, moving in a straight line for 30 frames at 2 to 80 mm per second, with the echo phase advancing with axial motion. Brightness is a multiple of the local noise. These runs used the first 512 frames.
  4. Phase-screen test over 29,996 tracked bubble positions in 8 mm patches, with split-half, null and injected-screen controls: 0.13 to 0.22 radians and no gain above 2.6 cm; 0.4 to 0.7 radians and about 1 dB in five of eight patches below.
  5. The code and every table behind these numbers are in a private repository; ask me for access. I wrote the analysis with Claude Code as a coding assistant, and every number comes from runs of that code.
